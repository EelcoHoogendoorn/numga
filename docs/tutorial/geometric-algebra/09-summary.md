# 9. Summary

Everything in chapters 1 to 8 follows from one change to the scalar product of vectors: two different basis vectors no longer multiply to zero, but their products in the two orders sum to zero. Nothing else was defined. From that change alone:

- The geometric product of two vectors keeps the scalar product as its scalar part, and adds bivector terms, three of them in three dimensions.
- Each bivector squares to $-1$. In three dimensions the scalars and bivectors form an algebra of their own, with the algebraic structure of the quaternions.
- The product of three different basis vectors is a trivector, which also squares to $-1$.
- Any product of basis vectors reduces, up to sign, to a product of different basis vectors, one for each choice of which basis vectors appear. In three dimensions these are eight basis elements, closed under the geometric product: the whole algebra of three-dimensional space.

The chapters so far work in three dimensions, but the multiplication rules of the geometric product do not mention a dimension. With any number $n$ of basis vectors, they apply unchanged. Every product of basis vectors still reduces, up to sign, to a product of different basis vectors, and there is one such product for each choice of which basis vectors appear: $2^n$ basis elements, closed under the geometric product. What is particular to three dimensions are the counts and what follows from them: three bivectors, a single trivector, and the scalars and bivectors closing on their own, with the structure of the quaternions. From four dimensions on, the product of two bivectors such as $xy$ and $zw$ is a product of four basis vectors.
